Twenty-four words, one French book, 0.2024 BTC.

In August 2024 the author of a French book about energy and money hid a bitcoin prize behind a 24-word seed phrase. Twenty-one words are in the book. Fourteen clues were published. Two years later the coins have still not moved.

This is the story of one attempt: one person and a crew of AI assistants, four days of reading, arguing and brute force, 160 logged searches over 5.0 trillion candidate phrases, and not a single hit. We didn't win (yet), so here is everything we learned.

01 · The setup

A book, an address, and a wallet nobody can open

The book is Pierre Noizat's L'énergie, face cachée de la monnaie (2024). On 15 August 2024 a single transaction sent 0.2024 BTC to a native SegWit address, bc1q8zfy…pgc02, at the first receive path m/84'/0'/0'/0/0. Whoever rebuilds the 24-word phrase can spend it.

The rules are strict. All 24 words are on the official BIP39 word lists. Words 1 to 21 are French and printed in the book; each is swapped for the English word at the same line number of the list. Words 22 to 24 are English. The clues were drip-fed between August and December 2024, and a publisher's LinkedIn post in 2026 brought a fresh wave of hunters, many with AI tools.

02 · The check

Eight words that work like a lie detector

A 24-word phrase carries 264 bits: 256 bits of key plus an 8-bit checksum, the first byte of the SHA-256 of those 256 bits. Words 1–23 hold 253 key bits, so the last word carries the final 3 key bits plus the checksum. No clue gives those 3 bits, so from our side they can be any of 8 values, and each value gives a different valid last word.

The setter did something clever. He gave eight different words that are all valid in slot 24 for the right first 23 words: alien detect flip gas organ peasant staff trigger. Their list positions fall one in each block of 256, which pins down all 8 checksum bits under all 8 settings of the 3 free bits. A wrong 23-word guess passes all eight by chance about once in 264. So we never needed the blockchain to test a guess: one hash per candidate and a hit is proof.

The final 24th word is not one of the eight and breaks the checksum on purpose, so it has to come from the clue, not from math. That part was the easiest: Samsung's 2024 Galaxy campaign with the K-pop group TXT was called Open Always Wins.

05 · The tools

From a Python loop to fifteen times faster C

The checker itself is tiny: pack 253 bits, hash, compare one byte for each of the eight check words. The work was in making it fast and making sure it was right.

bf → bf4

Plain C brute-forcers over word pools. bf4 moved to 128-bit integers after we found that six-word groups (66 bits) silently overflowed 64-bit math. Eight runs had to be thrown away.

bf5 → bf6

SHA-256 with the CPU's SHA-NI instructions, about 7 times faster. Measured around 41.5 million candidates a second on one cloud box.

bf7

OpenSSL's hashing turned out about 15 times faster again inside cloud containers. A 300-billion box now takes about an hour.

Positive controls

From day two, every run starts by planting a known phrase and checking the tool finds it. Before that, a bug could pass as a miss.

Independent oracle audit

Two outside libraries (mnemonic and bip-utils) re-derived the word lists, checksum and first BIP84 address. They agreed with our engines on every check.

The work graph

All clues, answer options and 160 runs as one graph, so we could see which combinations were truly untested and which only felt that way.

06 · What we got wrong

The tools were sound. The reasoning wasn't always.

Halfway through we stopped and wrote a post-mortem against the raw files instead of our summaries. Every engine checked out. The mistakes were in how we thought.

We mistook a fit for a proof

One picture rule was the only one we had written down that gave exactly 14 words. We called it "locked by counting" and froze sixteen words on it, then spent most compute on the words that were easy to open instead of the ones we were least sure of.

"Every single change missed, so two blocks are wrong"

It sounded like a deduction and drove the whole plan. It wasn't: the single-change tests were never exhaustive, and one wrong rule can move two blocks at once.

We mixed readings that contradict each other

Our biggest run combined a picture rule that drops repeated words with a cover reading that keeps one. No single setter would do both. By our own numbers that run had a 3-in-4 chance of missing before it started.

Two standards of evidence side by side

We demanded that one word be printed in the book, and accepted two neighbours that never are. The clue says all 21 French words are in the book. That clash is now our weakest link.

Five agreeing threads were one opinion

Several AI threads "re-confirmed" the same reading from the same notes. Only a blind re-extraction was independent, and it said it could not justify a unique answer. Confidence moved with no new test behind it.

07 · What's left

The answer probably needs three changes at once

The graph says the core reading and every one-block change around it are fully tested. What remains untested mostly needs three blocks changed together, or a word that sits outside every option list we wrote down.

The biggest single assumption is the bridge from the author's biography to the Olympic years. If the two year words are printed in the book, as the clue says all French words are, they cannot be the usual "year as list rank" reading. Relaxing that one step reopens about 30% of everything we still think plausible.

Our standing rules going forward: assume the setter made no mistakes, assume the e-book is enough, and when nothing matches, one of our own assumptions is wrong. Massive compute has failed for two years. This puzzle rewards thinking differently, not hashing faster.

08 · How we worked

One human, many AI threads, one shared notebook

The hunt ran as a project of parallel AI threads (Claude) plus a second agent (Codex), coordinated by one person who mostly checked in from a phone. Everything went into one repository: a single reasoning file as the source of truth, a ledger of every tested combination, and a lab notebook with a hypothesis, a prediction and a result for each test.

The human's job turned out to be the hardest one: saying no to more brute force, asking for reasons instead of searches, and insisting that we think like a French puzzle setter.
